Coastlines

The slow tide

A year of walking the same half mile of shore with a notebook, and what it taught one surveyor about measuring a line that will not hold still.

Words and drawings from the notebook · 9 minute read

High 05:40Low 11:55High 18:10

The first thing you learn is that the shoreline on the map is a decision. Somebody chose a tide, a season and a morning, drew where the water stood, and everyone since has treated that line as the edge of the land. Stand on the sand for an hour and you watch the edge walk forty paces inland and think nothing of it.

The stretchHalf a mile of shingle and sand between two groynes, walked on 61 mornings. A sample essay written for this demo.

I began keeping the notebook because the numbers I was paid to produce felt too certain. A coordinate to the centimetre, for a boundary that moves with the moon. So I walked the same stretch whenever I could, and wrote down where the wet sand ended.

Measured from the same painted mark on the northern groyne, with a tape, to the highest line of damp sand. Crude, repeatable, and good to about a pace.

OneWhat a line leaves out

A line has no width, and a beach is nothing but width. Between the highest weed and the lowest ripple there is a strip that belongs to both sea and land for part of every day. Plants know this. So do the birds, who work the strip in shifts as it opens and closes.

The notebook made the strip visible. Within a month the entries stopped looking like errors around a true value and started looking like the thing itself: a band, breathing twice a day, wider at the new and full moon.

The map shows where the water was. The notebook shows where it lives.

Seasons moved the whole band. Winter storms dragged the sand down the beach and left it steep and narrow; by August it had crept back, flat and generous. None of this is news to anyone who has lived beside the sea. It was news to my spreadsheets.

Why it mattersFlood lines, property edges and footpaths are all drawn from one chosen tide.

TwoDrawing a band instead

So the question changed. Not “where is the edge?” but “how much of the time is this ground under water?” That question has an honest answer at every point on the beach, and the answer is a shade rather than a stroke.

The maps I draw now have a soft margin where the old ones had a hard one. People find them harder to argue with, oddly. A hard line invites a dispute about which side of it you stand on. A band admits what everyone on the beach can already see.

The band is drawn from the share of observed tides that reached each contour, not from a forecast.

I still walk the stretch. The notebook is on its third volume. The line on the official chart has not moved, and the sea has not once agreed with it.
